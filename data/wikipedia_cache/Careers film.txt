Careers  is a 1929 American all-talking pre-Code drama film directed by John Francis Dillon and produced and released by First National Pictures. It stars Billie Dove and features Antonio Moreno, Thelma Todd, and Noah Beery. The film was based on a 1924 German play entitled Karriere, written by Alfred Schirokauer and Paul Rosenhayn.
Careers was Billie Dove's first film with dialogue.

Plot
Set in a remote and oppressive French colonial outpost in Cochinchina (then part of French Indochina), the story centers on Victor Duval, an ambitious young magistrate serving as a substitute judge in what feels like a true exile. He lives with his devoted and strikingly beautiful wife, Helene, who despairs over the lack of distraction and the couple’s stagnant life. Victor has rendered relevant services but waits in vain for a promotion that would transfer him to a less troubled and boring place.
The couple hopes to endure a few difficult years abroad until Victor earns a long-awaited promotion. However, Victor’s advancement depends not on merit but on the favor of the colony’s powerful Resident (a corrupt official distinct from the Governor). Carouge,  a distinguished Parisian attorney—tells Helene a painful truth: the Resident only proposes an official’s promotion when he takes an interest in that official’s wife. Promotions depend less on professional merit than on the “amiability” of the officials’ wives. Helene, a woman of strong moral character, refuses to believe in such infamy.
At the colony’s annual banquet, where promotions are publicly announced, Victor confidently expects recognition due to his excellent professional record, particularly a recent successful case. Instead, he is humiliated: not only is he passed over, but a less capable man is promoted in his place. Determined to seek justice, Victor decides to bypass colonial hierarchy and travel to the capital to appeal directly to the Governor.
Carouge later reveals the full truth to Helene: her husband’s failure is not due to lack of merit, but because she was “too virtuous” with the Resident. In fact, her refusal has not only blocked Victor’s promotion but has led to his demotion to an insignificant post.
Shocked and guilt-ridden, Helene resolves to act without telling her husband. She seeks out the Resident in his office to ask why he failed to propose Victor’s promotion to the Paris government. As soon as he sees the beautiful Helene, the Resident puts on the airs of a Don Juan or conqueror, enveloping her in flirtatious compliments. He dismisses his secretary, leaving them alone, and lets himself be carried away by a hallucinatory amorous rapture, trying to subjugate her to his whims. During this encounter, Helene inadvertently reveals Victor’s plan to approach the Governor (the higher colonial authority in the capital) to appeal directly for justice. Realizing this threatens his control, the Resident immediately attempts to telephone the Governor to sabotage Victor’s appeal.
Desperate, Helene begs the Resident not to harm her husband’s prospects. The Resident coldly informs her that Victor’s fate now depends entirely on her willingness to yield to him. He offers to reverse his damaging report and ensure Victor’s promotion—if she complies. As he presses his advantage, becoming increasingly aggressive and even violent, the situation suddenly changes.
Precisely at that moment, the Resident discovers, behind a folding screen, a native man (Sojin) who had hidden there to steal some valuable jewels. The two engage in a fierce struggle, ending with the native killing the Resident before Helene’s stunned and horrified eyes. The intruder then locks the room and turns on Helene, attempting to assault her. Fighting back, she seizes a small weapon (a penknife) and wounds him before managing to escape.
The Resident’s murder sends shockwaves through the colony. Beginning the investigations to determine the causes and perpetrators of the crime, the local authorities assign Judge Victor Duval himself—unaware of his wife’s involvement—to unravel the mystery. The Governor arrives to oversee the crisis. The arrested native accuses a “white woman” of being the murderess, hoping to deflect blame. Victor arranges a confrontation between the native and all the white women of the colony. With deep sorrow, he sees the wretch point directly to Helene as the author of the crime.
However, after a painful nervous crisis, Helene clarifies everything. She fully defends herself before the justice system’s accusations, but she must also defend herself before her husband’s eyes, who can find no explanation for why she was in the Resident’s office. She explains her visit: she sought out the Resident to beg him not to delay Victor’s promotion, never yielded to his coercion, and the Resident’s death occurred before any compromise could take place. Her eloquence and sincerity convince Victor that Helene has not ceased to be the same honest and worthy wife he has always trusted.
In the legal proceedings that follow, Helene is fully exonerated. The evidence confirms that the Resident was killed by the native intruder, who is convicted and punished. Helene’s actions are understood as self-defense and a desperate attempt to protect her husband. Although her account proves true, the revelation that she was willing to risk her honor—and even her safety—for his advancement deeply wounds Victor’s pride and sense of integrity. Gradually, reconciliation replaces bitterness. Victor comes to recognize both Helene’s sacrifice and the corruption of the system that placed them in such a position. Their marriage, though tested, endures.
In the aftermath, Victor’s future takes a decisive turn. Victor finally receives the long-delayed promotion that will allow the couple to return to Europe. Carouge—impressed by Victor’s integrity and Helene’s courage—offers him a prestigious legal partnership in Paris, providing an honorable escape from the moral compromises of colonial life. Happy and at peace, Victor and Helene resume their old life of tranquility, leaving the colony behind. Their bond strengthened by hardship, they look forward to a new beginning in Europe—free from the corruption, danger, and injustices that nearly destroyed them.

Music
The film featured a theme song entitled "I Love You — I Hate You (For Making a Fool of Me)," with words by Al Bryan and music by George W. Meyer. Carmel Myers sings the song during the film and it is also heard in the background in several scenes as part of the instrumental scoring.
A song entitled "My Sweet Helene," also written by Bryan and Meyer, was originally sung as a duet between Antonio Moreno and Billie Dove. This song was cut from the film after preview audiences reacted unfavorably. The only reference to the song remaining is during the last scene when Antonio Moreno exclaims "My Sweet ..." before kissing Billie Dove at the final fade out. Nevertheless, sheet music survives for this song survives as it was released before the final version of the film was edited.
During the party sequence at the Resident's headquarters an unidentified classical number is performed by Andrés de Segurola.

Censorship
When Careers was released in the United States, many states and cities in the United States had censor boards that could require cuts or other eliminations before the film could be shown. The Chicago Board of Censors passed the film on a "pink" or "adults only" basis for showing at the Chicago Theatre.

Preservation
The film was considered a lost film, with only the Vitaphone soundtrack still in existence. However, a print of the silent version was discovered in an Italian film archive in 2017. The all-talking version remains still lost.
